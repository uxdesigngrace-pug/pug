A Century of Change: an exhibit and kiosk about U.S. climate

Climate data you can walk through.

A 40-by-30-foot gallery where the visitor path runs from 1925 at the entry to the present day at the exit, and the data narrows to your own city.

SSituation
§01

Making a century of climate data tangible

Climate change data can be difficult to understand through charts and numbers alone. I developed a concept for an interactive museum exhibit using spatial design, storytelling, and a digital kiosk to present 100 years of U.S. climate trends. Visitors could start with a national view and explore climate changes in their own city or other locations, creating a more engaging and personalized learning experience.

A kiosk assignment using NOAA climate data grew into a four-part exhibition concept: an entrance pillar, curved timeline wall, interactive touchscreen table, and closing panel. I connected the physical visitor route with a national-to-local information path and considered circulation and reach early in the design.

TTask & research
§02

Research that shaped the visitor journey

I used the museum's public materials — including the Lights Out exhibition and its visual description tour — as contextual references. I did not visit the site or use architectural drawings. The 40-by-30-foot gallery footprint is my proposed design.

I used NOAA climate datasets and publicly available Smithsonian brand guidance. To guide the design, I considered three accessibility scenarios: seated or wheelchair use, Spanish-language preference, and listening rather than reading. These scenarios informed the proposed table height and interface controls.

AAction & design
§03

From national context to a local story

The kiosk starts with a national view. Visitors can then select a region, state, and city to explore climate data relevant to them. I kept the chart layouts consistent across screens so visitors wouldn't need to relearn how to read the information.

§04

Designing a journey through the gallery

I kept the timeline's 1925–2025 intervals equally spaced to avoid distorting the climate story. The curved wall and planned caption heights support reading along the visitor route; the attract pillar is designed for legibility from the corridor.

§05

Accessibility before visual polish

Accessibility considerations shaped the proposed spatial concept before visual styling: a planned 5-foot circulation path, an open-sided table with knee clearance, and a screen center at 1100 mm. The kiosk concept also includes English and Spanish choices, text sizing, audio, and redundant non-color cues.

§06

A visual language across the experience

The visual system uses orange for temperature and blue for water in the kiosk charts. The wall's temperature scale changes from blue to orange, so a separate legend distinguishes its meaning. Colors and typography reference the Smithsonian's publicly available branding guidance.

§07

The choices behind the experience

  • Start nationally, then explore locally.

    I used Miami as an example to demonstrate what the final city-level screen would look like. The kiosk begins with a national introduction, then allows visitors to select a region, state, and city to explore local climate trends.

  • Use a curved layout to guide visitors.

    The original symmetrical layout felt more like a showroom than an interactive exhibit. I introduced a curved timeline wall to create a more immersive experience and guide visitors through the years 1925 to 2025.

  • Prioritize accessibility from the beginning.

    I established accessibility requirements before developing the visuals, including a 5-foot pathway clearance and a 1,100 mm screen height. These requirements shaped the layout rather than being added afterward.

§08

The principles guiding the exhibit

I worked through Garrett's Elements of User Experience from the top down: the strategy came from people treating climate data as someone else's problem unless it's local, the scope became four spaces with one job each, and the structure was the national-to-city path. Hick's Law is behind that path: five regions, then a few states, then a few cities. On the UX Honeycomb, a compressed time axis would have made a more dramatic wall and a less credible one, so I got the drama from scale and placement instead.

ROutcome & reflection
§09

What remains a concept, and what comes next

This was the first time I designed a space and not only a screen. Working in feet and inches meant thinking about bodies: how tall people are, where a wheelchair goes, what you can read from 15 feet away. It was also the first project where I set the accessibility numbers before drawing anything, and the layout had to fit around them, which made some decisions easier.

As a concept, it still has next steps. The 6-foot standoff is an estimate, and the closing panel copy is still a placeholder. If the Peak-End Rule is right, the end is one of the moments people remember most, and right now it's the least finished part. Before trusting the table height, I'd want to put a prototype in front of people, including at least one wheelchair user.
