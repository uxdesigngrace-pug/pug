The 60-second case study

From fragmented data to connected matchmaking.

SupplierMatch had to connect buyers, certified suppliers, platform staff, and program partners in one experience. My design challenge was not simply creating screens: it was giving each role a clear path through search, matching, meeting coordination and follow-up, while maintaining a consistent system underneath.

01

Unify the underlying language

Consolidated overlapping industry fields and clarified the NAICS-based taxonomy that powers discovery and matching.

02

Make matches actionable

Connected ranked results to supplier profiles, conversations and a three-time-slot meeting workflow, with role-specific status and notifications.

03

Design for what happens next

Extended the system to onboarding, event operations, staff permissions and dashboards; used post-event behavioral evidence to identify follow-up improvements.

Scope: Four user groups · 166 screens mapped · cross-platform flows. Evidence: project artifacts and post-event behavioral observations; precise business outcome metrics are not reported here.

Ssituation

Ssituation01the tension

Buyers could not find them, suppliers could not be found

SupplierMatch matches corporate buyers with certified minority-owned suppliers. It is built by In Time Tec. NMSDC uses it as a matchmaking tool. I designed the product end to end — taxonomy, search, scheduling, notifications, mobile and the design system.

It runs two ways. One is open matchmaking across the certified supplier base. The other is an enterprise instance: a company's own supplier network brought in and turned into a matching engine, so internal buyers are matched against the suppliers that organization already works with.

The data underneath was tangled either way — three overlapping industry fields and more than a thousand NAICS codes — and the team was small. Every decision traded the ideal against what could ship.

Ssituation02who it's for

Four groups, one platform

SupplierMatch had to work for three groups with different jobs, often in the same room on the same day. A corporate buyer with a project to place. A certified supplier trying to reach that buyer without cold outreach. And the event admin running matchmaking for a whole conference floor.

Behind those three sits a fourth: the partner organization that runs its own program on SupplierMatch and shares in the revenue it brings in. It rarely meets the others, but every number it sees is built from what they do.

They come in at different doors and leave with different things. Buyers see Projects, suppliers see Opportunities, and the admin sees the floor. The same meeting has to read three ways, which is why the vocabulary underneath had to be settled before any of these screens could be drawn.

Dana, Marcus and Priya are illustrative. They are a way to hold three sets of needs in view while designing one system — not findings from interviews.

Ttask

Ttask03the brief

One product, four groups, one taxonomy

My task was to design SupplierMatch end to end: the buyer app, the supplier app, the admin dashboard with its Owner, Admin and Sales roles, the partner dashboard and its revenue, the emails, Suma and the public site, all running on one shared classification system.

The constraints were a small team, data that did not agree with itself, and a fixed date — matchmaking had to be ready for the 2026 NMSDC Annual Conference & Exchange.

Four stories, and I designed all of them

Every screen in this case study belongs to one group’s story, and I designed each story end to end — the buyer’s, the supplier’s, the admin team’s and the partner’s — rather than a slice of one.

Aaction

Aaction05taxonomy

Before any screen, I fixed the language

Three conflated industry fields became one NAICS-based system: one Primary code, one Secondary, and up to four Other. Nothing on screen works until the words underneath agree.

Typing filters the codes live. Choosing one drops it into the Primary slot with a soft settle, and the next slot unlocks.

Aaction06the map

Six areas, one hundred and sixty-six screens

Two audiences, two side navigations, and a platform that has to hold both without either one seeing the other's product. I mapped the whole thing before building it: the public website, shared account access, the buyer app, the supplier app, the admin and partner dashboards, and the system emails.

Buyers see Projects. Suppliers see Opportunities. The same meeting appears in both, named for whoever is looking at it. Flows that only make sense with room — submitting a project, completing a supplier profile — are marked desktop-only rather than crushed into a phone.

The map is also how scope got cut. You cannot argue about what to drop until you can see all of it at once.

B
The buyer’s storydescribe a need → ranked matches → connect → meet
ch. 07–14

Aaction07directory

Buyers search differently, so search got three doors

Explore, Find and Directory are three ways into the same supplier base. Directory groups by category with real pagination, because infinite scroll quietly buries good suppliers below the fold.

The segment toggle slides its pill and the cards re-shuffle with a staggered fade. Corporate Plus suppliers stay mixed in rather than being floated to the top.

Aaction08location

I removed the fake precision

A search radius only means anything measured from a street address. Anywhere else it is a number that looks exact and is not. So there is one location field and one “search by” selector: ZIP, city, county and state are exact matches on headquarters, with no radius at all.

Choosing a street address reveals the radius ladder and expands a ring on the map. Any other type hides the radius entirely.

Aaction09first login

Two short videos, then out of the way

A buyer’s first visit to the dashboard opens a two-part walkthrough: how to reach the Help Center and live chat, then how to schedule a meeting. Each is one short video with a Skip, so nobody is held hostage by a tour.

The dashboard behind it does the rest. The buyer’s table sits at the top — Table 24 at the Matchmaking Hub — and below it are the three ways in: the attendee directory, supplier search, and project-based matching, where Suma ranks suppliers against what the buyer described. The videos stay one click away afterwards.

After a sync with NMSDC the table became a “one-on-one meeting table”, and the number repeats on My Meetings, so the buyer reads the same words wherever it appears.

Aaction10the match

Matches arrive on the project, not in an inbox

A buyer’s matches belong to a project, so that is where they show up. Each card on My Projects carries the project’s keywords, value, industry category and codes, and a Matches button with a count on it — the count is the notification.

I drew two versions of the project card and the matches page and compared them side by side. This is version B.

Aaction11the shortlist

A ranked shortlist the buyer can check

Opening Matches shows every supplier ranked by a match percentage from Suma, with the reasons underneath: keywords, supplier type, category, designations and NAICS codes. A score on its own is a number to trust or ignore. A score next to its evidence is something a buyer can check.

Status tabs follow the relationship — Matches, Applied, Connections, Disconnected, Awarded — and Connect and Award sit on every card. Matching Criteria is one button away, with the last update time beside it, so a buyer who disagrees with the list can change what it is built on.

Removing a supplier is forgiving: the card collapses and a toast offers Undo for five seconds. The whole list can also be sent to email or downloaded.

Aaction12the match card

One card, ten moods

The supplier card is the product’s atom — it appears in search, in the directory, in projects and in messages. I specced ten states — default, hover, selected, focus, pinned, meeting scheduled, awarded, not accepting, collapsed and loading — so one component could carry every stage of a relationship.

On a match it carries everything a buyer needs to decide: the Corporate Plus badge, locations, keywords, supplier type, category, industries, revenue, designations, NAICS codes and the value proposition, with the match percentage and Connect and Award at the bottom.

Aaction13connect

Anonymous until you choose not to be

Connect opens one choice, not a form: message the supplier anonymously, or schedule a meeting. Buyers stay anonymous until they decide to share who they are.

Scheduling asks the buyer for three times and the supplier picks one, which removes the back-and-forth without taking the choice away from either side.

Aaction14messages

Conversations without leaving work

Messages open as a docked panel rather than a page, so a buyer never loses their place mid-search. Suma, the AI assistant, shares that same space — but never at the same time as the inbox.

Opening Messages tucks Suma away with a cross-slide, and a “Give Suma a break” toggle dims the assistant rather than removing it.

Aaction15a head start

Fifty details, already filled in

Suppliers used to meet a blank form. Now their profile starts from their NMSDC certification: the record fills in fifty details before they arrive, and those fields are locked, so buyers can rely on them.

The first screen says so up front — we’ve filled in 50 details for you — and each of the four steps shows how much of it is already done: thirty-seven in contact and company details, eight in offerings and locations, five in additional information. What is left is the part only the supplier can write.

Inside a step, a pinned progress bar keeps their place, and locked fields are greyed with a padlock in front of the label so it never reads as the required marker. Progress saves at every step, on a phone or a computer.

I designed it for the phone first, this week. It replaces an earlier call to have suppliers build their profile from scratch.

Aaction16the other side

Suppliers see the match from their side

Where a buyer has Projects, a supplier has Opportunities. Every project they were matched to shows up with its locations, industries, estimated value, status, timeline and a match percentage from Suma.

The tabs follow the relationship from the supplier’s end — Matches, Applied, Connected, Closed, Awarded — so a match is the first step on a path rather than an alert with nowhere to go.

Aaction17the supplier dashboard

Showing suppliers what being matched looks like

Most of the projects a supplier is evaluated against never reach them, because they rank outside what the buyer sees. A concept for the supplier dashboard turns that into something they can use: how many opportunities their profile was evaluated against this week, the active projects and their value in the supplier’s categories, and new buyers in their industry.

The profile-strength panel ties every suggestion to demand — add a healthcare case study, because 23 active projects in your categories ask for one — so improving a profile is an answer to buyers rather than a chore.

It is a concept, not a shipped screen, and the near-miss insight is marked coming soon.

Aaction18the request

The supplier hears about it first

On the supplier’s side the request lands on the dashboard under Requests, marked New Request: the project, the buyer (still anonymous), the project value, the meeting length, the buyer’s table at the Matchmaking Hub, and the three proposed times.

Two actions, nothing else: review it, or request a new time.

Aaction19accept

Accepting is one choice

Review opens the three proposed times with the table and venue above them. The supplier picks one and confirms. There is nothing else to fill in.

Aaction20the meeting

Then the meeting happens

Confirmed meetings move onto the supplier’s dashboard, grouped by day: the time and length, the company, where it is — Matchmaking Hub, Table 24 — and the contact’s name, phone and email.

Download my schedule puts the whole conference on one page for the day itself.

Aaction21after the conference

Suppliers stopped hunting for their meetings

Clarity recordings from the days after the conference showed the supplier dashboard had the most quick backs on the site — thirty-one. Suppliers landed, left for Meetings within seconds, then clicked Upcoming, Ongoing and Completed looking for theirs.

The proposal puts a post-conference banner on top — follow up with the buyers you met — and four shortcuts with counts underneath: Requests, Upcoming, Completed and Messages, so nobody tab-hops to find out whether they have anything to do.

The admin team’s storyevents, tables, logs · Owner, Admin and Sales
ch. 22–25

Aaction22on the floor

Thousands of people, one weekend

SupplierMatch also runs the matchmaking at conferences and buyer-supplier events. Buyers sign up by QR code and suppliers verify a registration code to accept, and all of it has to work on a phone held in one hand in a crowded hall.

The drawer slides in from the right with a navy pill on the active item. Messages and Suma float as buttons and open as partial sheets, never taking the whole screen.

Aaction23the event

Who is here, and who is ready

Inside an event, buyers and suppliers sit on separate tabs. The counts across the top answer the questions an admin gets on the day: how many are attending, how many have activated their profile, and how many still have no table.

Registrants come in by file import rather than one at a time. Every row carries two statuses, table and profile, because a buyer with a table and no activated account still misses their meetings. Selecting a row opens the seat map beside the list.

The admin side kept going after the conference. In the days after, Clarity recordings showed admins clicking the Pending and Past counts on the meeting log as if they were filters — seventeen dead clicks. I proposed folding the counts and the segment control into one row of status tabs, so each count is the filter.

Aaction24roles and permissions

One back office, three kinds of staff

SupplierMatch’s own team is not one kind of user, so the admin side is built around three roles — Owner, Admin and Sales — and each one sees the tools for its job and nothing that could break someone else’s.

The Owner holds everything: billing and payouts, and the team itself — inviting staff, changing roles, removing access. Admin runs the network day to day: suppliers and buyers, events and tables, the meeting and registration logs. Sales works the accounts: partner and company accounts, plans and the revenue view, with the network read-only.

Permissions are shown rather than hidden. The selected person’s role sits beside the team list as a matrix of Edit, View and Locked, and a locked action keeps its place with a padlock instead of disappearing, so nobody has to wonder where a button went.

Aaction25the admin dashboard

Nine nav items became six

The first admin dashboard grew one page at a time, and it showed: nine nav items, three identical line charts, a dropdown on every chart, and the same Suppliers and Buyers blocks on two pages.

I consolidated it. Analytics folded into Overview, Suppliers and Buyers became tabs under Network, and subscriptions, revenue share and affiliates became tabs under Revenue. Events and the meeting and registration logs sit together under Activity. One filter bar for period and industry replaces a dropdown per chart, and one KPI tile with three variants replaces three stat-card styles.

A new Needs attention card turns the numbers into jobs: canceled or rescheduled meetings, pending registrations, meeting requests with no reply, canceled subscriptions — each with a link to where it gets fixed.

It is a proposal, with the open questions written on the file for the product lead and the developers.

The partner’s storyprogram overview → revenue share → payouts

Aaction26the partner dashboard

The program at a glance

Behind the two apps sit two back offices with different access: SupplierMatch’s own admin, and the partner dashboard a partner organization uses to run its program.

The partner overview reports on the year: total awarded, number of matches, active projects and their value, suppliers and buyers with their top industries, and connections — meetings, active conversations and potential project value. Bulk upload brings an existing supplier list in at once.

Aaction27partner revenue

Revenue a partner can check, month by month

A partner runs its program on SupplierMatch and shares in the subscriptions its members pay for, so both back offices have a revenue side. The admin sees it as payouts; the partner sees it as its share.

The top row answers the first question: monthly recurring revenue, net revenue after the share, the current share rate and the payout to date, with subscriptions split into total, active, new and canceled. The trend draws underneath.

The revenue share tab answers the second — what do we get, and has it been paid — with one row per month: paid subscriptions, the payout and a Paid or Pending status.

The figures in the demo are placeholder data from the design file, not real program numbers.

Aaction28the AI assistant, now

Suma, the last stop before a human

This is what I am designing now. After the conference, most of what reached support was the same few questions — I never got my invitation link, how do I log in, can we use this for our own suppliers. So the login became one login for everyone, and Suma became the help at the end of it.

Suma answers with choices rather than a blank box: get support, book a demo, something else. Every branch ends in an action — resend my invitation link, a short message form, or Talk to our team — so a conversation always finishes somewhere.

The chat slides up from the corner, the reply shows a typing beat before it lands, and a chosen answer fills in before the next question arrives.

Aaction29routing

Designed around what each answer costs

The support desk charges for every automated answer, so Suma automates exactly one thing: a greeting and one short form — name, email, and how can we help.

Inside business hours, Monday to Friday, eight to five Mountain, Suma says an agent will join shortly and a person picks up the chat live. After hours the message is saved as a ticket, Suma says so, and the reply comes by email within one business day.

Either way the visitor knows what happens next before they close the chat. I drew it in lanes — the visitor, Suma, the system and support — so everyone could see who owns each step, and it is being set up in the support desk now.

Aaction30notifications

Nobody wants an empty email

The daily digest only sends when there is something worth saying, which is a content rule rather than a visual one and did more for trust than any layout decision.

Entering a mobile number opens a code field that advances digit by digit, then confirms.

Aaction31the emails

Seventeen emails, and the one that has to survive Outlook

The notification settings decide what goes out; the emails are what actually lands. Seventeen of them — six for buyers, eight for suppliers, and the account mail for password resets and one-time codes.

They are built as tables with inline styles, because that is what email clients still want, and branded NMSDC Matchmaker, powered by SupplierMatch. The two here are the supplier invite, which has to explain a whole new way of meeting buyers to someone who has never used the product, and the meeting confirmation, which has one job and an action deadline inside it.

Outlook blocks images by default, so the logos fall back to a text wordmark rather than leaving a broken box at the top of the message. An email that only works with images on is an email that does not work.

Aaction32the public face

Branding, and the front door

The product needed a public face before anyone could be sent to it: a marketing site that explains matchmaking to a buyer who has never heard of it, carries the In Time Tec endorsement, and holds the same system as the app — violet for buyers, teal for suppliers, navy for active states, cards on an eight-pixel radius.

Four pages shipped for launch: the home page, the enterprise story, the events page for conference matchmaking, and the company page.

Rresult

Rresult33what shipped

What shipped, and what I would do next

SupplierMatch launched with its four-page marketing site, and NMSDC Matchmaker, powered by SupplierMatch, ran the matchmaking for the 2026 NMSDC Annual Conference & Exchange in Los Angeles, October 25–28.

[Outcomes and metrics go here — registrants, meetings booked, matches accepted, and any numbers you are willing to publish.]

The work did not stop at the conference. Clarity recordings from the days after showed where people still struggled — dead clicks on the admin meeting log, and suppliers hopping between tabs to find their meetings — and both became the next round of proposals.

Next: the full connection flow, mobile profile editing, and linking suppliers to projects.

What I learned: scoping down is a design skill, and clear language does as much work as clear layout. The taxonomy chapter saved more design time than any screen in this case study.

The buyer’s story: Find qualified, certified suppliers fast, and meet them face to face.

  1. Describe the need. Dana from procurement submits a project with the NAICS codes that matter.
  2. See the matches. Suma surfaces certified suppliers that fit. Dana pins the strongest ones.
  3. Propose times. She proposes up to three times. Her name stays private until she shares it.
  4. Meet in person. They meet at Table 14 at the conference, with context already in hand.

The supplier’s story: Get in front of the right buyers without cold outreach.

  1. Get invited. Marcus gets an invite to conference matchmaking and registers.
  2. Build trust. He builds his profile. Certified details stay locked, so buyers can rely on them.
  3. Pick a time. A meeting request arrives. He picks the time that works and gets a text confirmation.
  4. Win the work. One well-prepared meeting turns into awarded work.

The event admin’s story: Run conference matchmaking without spreadsheets.

  1. Import attendees. Priya imports buyers and suppliers through separate uploads, so lists never mix.
  2. Assign tables. She assigns tables so every meeting has a place on the floor.
  3. Review requests. Profile change requests land in one log to approve or decline.
  4. Run the day. On the day, every meeting’s status sits in one view.
