the perspective behind the pixels

Not a straight line. A stronger perspective.

My career hasn't followed a straight line — and that's shaped how I design.

I didn't begin in UX. I began in healthcare, helping people make sense of unfamiliar systems, language barriers, and decisions that mattered to their lives.

Living across cultures and moving between industries taught me to question my own assumptions. I learned to see the context behind a user's frustration, not just the screen in front of them.

I bring more than design methods to a product. I bring real-life experiences that shape the questions I ask, the people I listen to, and the solutions I build.

the longer version

how i got here.

From patient care to UX and product design. Different tools, same purpose.

My journey into UX didn’t start with design software or perfectly arranged pixels. It started in an OB/GYN practice through a partnership with NewYork-Presbyterian Queens, where I worked with patients from low-income communities facing health literacy, language, and technology barriers.

Beyond health education, I helped patients navigate online applications, access medical records, and use digital healthcare services. I became the bridge between patients and technology, and realized that people weren’t struggling because they lacked ability. Often, the systems themselves were simply too complicated.

That made me wonder: what if I could improve the experience itself, instead of always helping people work around its problems?

That question led me to UX design.

After earning my degree in Community Health Education from York College, CUNY, I transitioned into design through Designlab’s UX/UI bootcamp and later pursued a master’s degree in User Experience at Arizona State University, deepening my passion for human-centered design, usability, and accessibility.

Today, I design public-sector and enterprise products at In Time Tec, including digital learning experiences for educators, students, and families. Whether I’m simplifying complex workflows, improving accessibility, or making information easier to understand, my purpose remains the same: helping people navigate technology with confidence.

I believe technology should adapt to people, not the other way around. I’m always striving to blend usability with beauty, creating experiences that are intuitive, inclusive, and enjoyable.

Looking ahead, I hope to bring my healthcare background and experience in UX, product design, and learning experience design closer together, especially in health technology, education, and care systems.

My goal is simple: to create products that make a real, meaningful impact on the lives of living beings.

Because great design isn’t just about how something looks or works. It’s about the difference it makes in someone’s life, including our four-legged friends. After all, animals are users too… they just have a harder time filling out usability surveys. 🐾

east ↔ west

seven cities, four countries.

Countless perspectives. Different places, different cultures, and a lifelong curiosity about people. I’ve called Jakarta, Singapore, Vancouver, New York City, Phoenix, Boise, and Austin home. Moving between cultures taught me that what feels natural to one person might be completely unfamiliar to another. Along the way, I developed a love for observing people, recognizing patterns, understanding different perspectives, and helping others achieve their goals.

01 · Jakarta, Indonesia

Where my curiosity began.

I was born in Indonesia to Chinese parents, so navigating different languages and cultures was part of my life from the very beginning. My grandmother spoke her own Chinese dialect, and I remember trying to understand everyone in the room, even when I couldn’t understand every word.

I became curious about how people expressed themselves through their words, gestures, and behaviors. I loved observing, finding patterns, and figuring out what people meant beyond what they said.

Looking back, I guess I was doing a little user research before I even knew UX existed.

That curiosity has stayed with me. I love understanding what drives people, respecting their differences, and finding ways to help them achieve better outcomes.

02 · Singapore

Different cultures, shared understanding.

Living in a multicultural environment taught me that communication isn’t just about language. It’s about listening, adapting, and understanding different perspectives.

03 · Vancouver, Canada

Learning what it feels like to start over.

Moving to Vancouver meant improving my English and adapting to unfamiliar systems. I learned firsthand how much clear communication and a little guidance can matter.

04 · New York City

Where empathy became a career.

New York was where I studied public health and worked with diverse patient communities. I learned to listen beyond people’s words and understand the challenges behind their needs.

05 · Phoenix, Arizona

Where curiosity became a career direction.

Phoenix was where I began my master’s journey in User Experience at Arizona State University, connecting my healthcare background with a growing passion for design and accessibility.

06 · Boise, Idaho

Where design became my everyday work.

Boise was where I started my UX/UI design career at In Time Tec, turning research, empathy, and creative ideas into real-world digital products.

07 · Austin, Texas

Bringing it all together.

Now Austin is home. I bring a little piece of every city into my work, along with the belief that good design begins with curiosity, empathy, and respect for different perspectives.

Seven cities later, I’m still observing, still learning, and still trying to make life a little easier for others.

what i believe

three things i keep coming back to.

01

Design for who gets missed

Public health trains you to ask who a system doesn't reach. A flow that holds up for someone on a screen reader, on bad wifi, at the end of a long day, holds up for everyone else too. It almost never works the other way round.

02

Impact, or just a word

I trust a number more than I trust the word impact. Completion rates, support tickets, time on task, who dropped out and where. If a decision helped someone, it shows up somewhere I can point to.

03

Shipped, or it didn't happen

A prototype nobody can use has helped nobody. I build the production version so the decisions survive the handoff — especially the accessibility ones, which are always first to be cut for time.
