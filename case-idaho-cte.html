SSituation

01 · the tension

The year runs on deadlines, not screens

PQM is the Performance Quality Metric: the standard Idaho's career and technical education programs are measured against. Around it sits a year of fixed windows — applications open 15 October and close 15 February, the LAA is due before 15 April, program review happens to whoever PQM selects, and a new program can be requested at any time.

None of that lived in one place. Before anything could be designed, the work had to be drawn: who does what, in what order, and where it stops to wait for someone else.

TTask & process

02 · the year

What the division's year actually looks like

Four processes carry dates. Two of them are windows with a hard open and close, one is triggered by selection rather than a calendar, and one is open all year. The rest hang off four standing tracks: standards, programs, funding and grants.

Drawn this way, the collisions are visible. The application window closes in February and the LAA falls due in April, which puts two of the division's heaviest processes inside eight weeks of each other.

AAction & service mapping

03 · the map

Eleven flows, 354 steps

The finished map holds 354 named steps, 39 decision points and 322 connectors. Grants alone is five programs under one roof: IQPS Incentive, IQPS Startup, PQI Establishment, PQI Technical Assistance and Program Quality Incentive.

Scale is the argument. A single approval screen is easy to draw; a system that has to carry a hundred and sixty-three steps of grant handling without losing anyone is a different problem, and the map is what makes that case.

04 · one process

One process, four organizations

Endorsement determination is the shortest flow on the board and still the most revealing. Eleven steps, two decisions, and four organizations: PQM, the Office of the State Board of Education, ISEE, and the Teacher Cert Team.

Watch where it changes hands. PQM decides whether the standards fit an existing endorsement, writes the language if they do not, and then the work leaves the building — OSBE approves, ISEE adds the code, and only then does the Teacher Cert Team learn a new program area exists.

Every handoff is a place the work can sit for weeks with nobody able to say where it is. That is the product problem, and it is not a screen problem.

05 · requirements

What the system has to do

The board states its own requirements, and they are about continuity rather than interface: version-controlled standards documentation with notifications when a standard changes, the ability to tie standards to programs, a way to establish and notify the Standards Writing Team, asynchronous collaborative review, industry surveying, and question-bank creation.

Standards themselves run a six-stage cycle, from planning through to cut-score determination a full year after implementation. A system that forgets what a standard said two revisions ago cannot support that cycle.

RResult & reflection

06 · what next

What I would do next, and what I cannot claim

The map is the deliverable this page can show. What it cannot show is outcome: I have no before-and-after cycle times, no error rates, and no adoption figures, so none are claimed here.

What I would measure, given the chance: how long a request sits at each handoff, how often a program is reviewed late because two windows collided, and how many times someone has to ask another organization where something is. The map already says where to put the instrumentation — it is the 39 decision points and every line that crosses a lane.
